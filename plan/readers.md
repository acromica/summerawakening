## Personas

### The critic
<!-- Reads erotic and transgressive fiction as literature. Asks: is this a novel that happens to be

- Reads: literary erotica and fiction about desire and power, e.g. Anaïs Nin's *Delta of Venus*, Pauline Réage's *Story of O*, Mary Gaitskill's *Bad Behavior*, André Aciman's *Call Me by Your Name*. [EDIT: swap in the books that set your own bar.]
- Rewards:
  - Desire rendered through specific perception: the texture of a fabric, the sound of a voice giving an instruction, the narrator noticing something about himself he didn't expect.
  - Sex scenes that change a relationship or reveal a character; a scene could not be cut without losing story.
  - A narrator whose naivety is shown through what he misreads, not announced.
  - Restraint: implication, the cut away, the sentence that stops one beat early.
  - The tension between shame and wanting kept alive rather than resolved too soon.
- Punishes:
  - Euphemism and porn vocabulary used as filler; the same few verbs and body words on repeat.
  - Interior monologue that explains the emotion the scene already delivered.
  - Characters who exist only to administer the next escalation.
  - Mannered prose, stock imagery, and anything that sounds like the banned list in `bible/style.md`.
  - Sentimental endings that tidy away the discomfort the book raised.
- Stops reading when: two consecutive scenes could be swapped without anyone noticing.

### The core reader

- Reads: feminization, crossdressing, and dominance/submission erotica, mostly self-published ebooks and online serials; erotic romance with power exchange. [EDIT: name 2–3 actual titles or authors you'd shelve this beside.]
- Comes for:
  - The slow, step-by-step transformation: each new garment, task, or rule is a beat, and the reader savors the escalation.
  - The power dynamic: the people who direct him are confident, specific, and enjoy it; the narrator's resistance gives way by degrees.
  - Humiliation and arousal tangled together, felt from inside the narrator's head.
  - The maid and gardener setup used fully: uniforms, chores, being watched, being corrected. [EDIT: which tropes are must-haves for you?]
  - Real heat on the page, delivered often enough to keep the promise of the genre.
- Puts the book down when:
  - The transformation stalls: three chapters without a new step.
  - Escalation jumps: he goes from first stockings to far beyond with no steps between, and the slow burn is lost.
  - The dynamic stops feeling consensual at the level of the fantasy: real cruelty or menace that kills the heat, rather than play. [EDIT: where is your line? Record it in premise.md as a hard limit.]
  - The book turns apologetic or clinical about the kink, or ridicules it.

### The skeptical general reader
<!-- Bright, busy, open-minded adult, not a genre fan; picked it up from a recommendation or
     because the title made them curious. Will give it one chapter. -->
- Reads: contemporary literary and upmarket fiction, e.g. Sally Rooney's *Normal People*, coming-of-age novels, the occasional book a friend insists on. [EDIT]
- Needs:
  - A narrator they like and want to follow before the explicit material arrives: someone funny, observant, or touching in his shyness.
  - Clear stakes beyond the next scene: what he stands to lose (job, self-image, a person) and what he wants besides sex.
  - A sense of the summer as a place: the house, the garden, the town, the heat.
  - Someone to care about besides the narrator, whose inner life is visible.
  - Movement toward love, as the logline promises, not just toward more sex.
- Puts the book down when:
  - The explicit scenes start to feel repetitive and the plot pauses to make room for them.
  - They can't tell who a new character is or why the narrator stays in a situation he says he hates.
  - The setup strains belief with nothing gained for it (why does a gardener become a maid?).
  - The narrator stays passive for too long with no choice that's his own.

- **Pull** — do I need to know what happens next, or want to stay in this world?
- **Clarity** — do I know who, where, when, and why, except where the book means me not to?
- **Feeling** — does it move me (fear, desire, grief, laughter) rather than tell me what to feel?
- **Character** — do the people feel alive, specific, and their choices their own?
- **Prose** — is the language precise, alive, and the author's own?
- **Texture** — specific perception, subtext, memorable detail, and ambiguity that feels intended rather than unclear?
- **Surprise and rightness** — are turns unexpected yet inevitable in hindsight?
- **Heat** — is the erotic charge built through tension, anticipation, and specific sensation rather than stated? n/a for chapters with no erotic content.
- **Escalation** — does this chapter take the narrator one believable step further (in body, role, or feeling) than the last, neither stalling nor skipping steps?
