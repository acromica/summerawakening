## 0. Contribution — what is this passage giving the reader? (always first)
Before asking whether a scene has a crisis or an arc beat, ask what it contributes to the experience this book intends (`plan/premise.md`, `plan/cycle.md`): pressure, discovery, a relationship deepening or souring, atmosphere, perception, comedy, pleasure in language, rest before a blow, a question planted.
- Name the contribution in a few words, with evidence. If you can name it and it's delivered, the scene is working on its own terms, even with no turn, no conflict, or no resolution.
- Judge the scene by whether the contribution is vivid and worth its length, and whether the surrounding sequence still moves. Comic, episodic, contemplative, and deliberately unresolved fiction are judged by what they attempt.
- Only when you can't name a contribution, or it's too thin for the scene's length, reach for §1–§4 to explain why.
- An agent-written scene card is a hypothesis about the scene, not a standard it must meet. Judge the scene, and say if the card was the wrong plan.

## 1. Scene function — does each scene turn?
Sources: Shawn Coyne, *The Story Grid* ("Five Commandments of Storytelling", storygrid.com); Dwight V. Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (scene and sequel); "try/fail cycles" as taught on *Writing Excuses*.
- **Value shift.** Name the value at stake (safety/danger, love/hate, truth/lie…) and its charge at the start and end. A scene whose value doesn't change is a candidate to cut or merge, even if it is well written.
- **Five commandments.** Can you point to the inciting incident, the progressive complications, the turning point, the crisis, the climax (the character's choice), and the resolution? A missing *crisis* (no real choice) is the most common reason a scene feels inert.
- **Crisis type.** Is the choice a *best bad choice* or *irreconcilable goods*? A choice with an obvious right answer isn't a crisis.
- **Complications escalate and vary.** Does each complication differ in kind and raise the cost? Repeated complications of the same kind read as padding.
- **Outcome.** Does the scene end in *yes, but* or *no, and*? A clean *yes* or a clean *no* ends tension; use them at structural turns, not routinely.
- **Scene and sequel.** After a disaster, is there room for reaction → dilemma → decision? Too little sequel leaves readers numb; too much makes it sag.
- **Polarity rhythm.** Across consecutive scenes, do the endings vary (up, down, worse, better)? Runs of the same polarity flatten a stretch of the book.

## 2. Promise, progress, payoff — why readers keep reading or quit
Sources: Brandon Sanderson, BYU creative-writing lectures (brandonsanderson.com, "Guide to Plot"; Odyssey Workshop talk); Mary Robinette Kowal, MICE quotient (*Writing Excuses*).
- **Promises.** What do the opening pages promise in tone, story, character/conflict, and genre? Does the book keep those promises, or quietly change them? A broken tone promise loses readers faster than a slow plot.
- **Progress signposts.** In each chapter, what tells the reader the story is moving: new information, a new obstacle, a decision, a shift in a relationship? Chapters with no signpost are where readers stop.
- **Payoff.** Is it surprising yet inevitable in hindsight? Is it earned (set up, paid for), and did the reader genuinely doubt it would come? Does it land on the book's emotional centre, not just its plot?
- **Thread type and nesting (MICE).** Classify each thread in `bible/threads.md` as **M**ilieu (enter/leave a place), **I**nquiry (question asked/answered), **C**haracter (who am I / change of self), or **E**vent (order disrupted/restored). Threads should close in roughly the reverse of the order they opened. An ending that resolves a different type of thread from the one the opening opened feels unsatisfying even when everything is "answered".

## 3. Character arc — want, need, and the lie
Sources: K. M. Weiland, *Creating Character Arcs* (helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com); Kurt Vonnegut's rules ("every character should want something").
- **Want vs. need.** What does the character want (usually external) and what do they need (usually an inner truth)? Are the two in conflict, so that getting the want alone would not satisfy them?
- **The lie.** What false belief keeps them chasing the want? Where is it shown on the page, and where is it tested?
- **The wound (ghost).** Is the lie rooted in something specific, shown or implied rather than explained?
- **Arc type.** Positive (they come to the truth), flat (they hold the truth and change others), or negative (they refuse it). Is the arc the book delivers the one the premise intends?
- **Choices, not events.** Does the character change through choices that cost them something, especially choices between want and need, rather than because things happen to them?
- **Every character wants something**, even minor ones, even if it's only a glass of water.

## 4. Escalation and the reader's mind
Sources: George Saunders, *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain* (page-by-page reading of the Russian masters); Kurt Vonnegut, "Eight Rules for Writing Fiction".
- **Always be escalating.** Does each beat raise or complicate what came before? A beat that merely repeats the situation is a stall, however nice its prose.
- **What has this page done to us?** After each page or beat: what does the reader now know, expect, worry about, or want? Good fiction raises an expectation, then complicates it rather than simply fulfilling or ignoring it.
- **Don't waste a stranger's time.** Is there a stretch that serves the author (research, backstory, set-dressing) but not the reader?
- **Rootability.** Is there someone the reader cares about, if not likes, early enough?

## 5. The fictive dream — what breaks immersion
Source: John Gardner, *The Art of Fiction* (psychic distance; the "vivid and continuous dream"; faults of technique and "of soul").
- **Psychic distance.** Gardner's ladder runs from far ("It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway.") to inside the character ("Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes…"). Flag *abrupt* jumps of more than a step or two that aren't deliberate, and passages stuck at one distance when the moment calls for closer or farther.
- **Distractions from the dream.** Clumsy or ambiguous sentences, needless explanation, a stray modern word in a period voice, impossible physical staging, an authorial wink.
- **Faults of soul.** *Sentimentality* (emotion asked for rather than earned), *frigidity* (the author holding the characters' pain at an ironic or clever distance), *mannerism* (style performing for its own sake, drawing attention away from the story).

## 6. Stimulus and response — line-level order
Source: Dwight V. Swain, motivation-reaction units.
- In charged moments, does the stimulus (what happens, shown objectively) come before the reaction, and does the reaction run feeling → reflex → action → speech? Reversed order ("She flinched as the glass shattered") blunts the impact.
- Is the reaction proportionate to the stimulus and true to this character?

## 7. Surface tics readers notice
Source: Elmore Leonard, "Ten Rules of Writing" (written for his own crime fiction; flags, not laws). `scripts/stats` counts the measurable ones.
- Dialogue tags other than "said", and adverbs modifying "said".
- "Suddenly", "all hell broke loose", and exclamation marks (Leonard: two or three per 100,000 words).
- Openings with weather; prologues; heavy dialect spelling.
- Long description of a character's appearance or of places the reader will skim. "Leave out the part that readers tend to skip."

## Using these in a report
- Name the lens briefly ("no crisis: the choice has an obvious answer"), then quote the text. Don't lecture on the framework.
- Rank findings by what the reader would feel, not by how many lenses they trip.
- When a lens conflicts with `bible/style.md` or a deliberate choice in `decisions.md`, the book wins. Don't report it.
