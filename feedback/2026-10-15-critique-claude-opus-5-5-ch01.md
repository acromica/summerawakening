## Priority findings

### 1. A repeated "It's X. / It's Y. / It looks like Z." cadence makes the description sound mechanical
- **What a reader experiences:** each new object arrives in the same stack of one-line fragments: "It's small. / It's very bright. / It looks like something a girl wears to prom." (63–67); "It's silver. / It has red glass in the eye sockets. / It looks like something you'd wear to a fight." (81–85); "It's blue. / It's a little short in the arms." (246–248); "It's heavier. / It doesn't stick out at the ends." (277–279); "It's white. / It has more windows…" (319–321); "It's gold, with a row of small blue stones." (621); "It's big and white and full of shade. … It looks cool in there. / It looks very far away." (650–658); "They're pale. / They're clean." (689–691). By the third repetition a reader hears the pattern instead of seeing the object. `scripts/stats` puts the mean line at 8.0 words, with 37% of paragraphs ending on a fragment; your exemplars run 15.7 words and 6%.
- **Likely cause:** I followed "prefer short sentences" by chopping description into separate lines, rather than writing short sentences that each do different work.
- **Suggested remedy:** keep the first pair (prom vs. fight, 67/85). That contrast is the point of the scene. Rewrite most of the others as one or two sentences of varied shape, e.g. fold the shirt into "my one good shirt, blue, a little short in the arms," and merge the house description into the drive sentence.
- **Confidence:** high that the repetition is there; medium on how far to loosen it, which depends on the voice you want.

### 2. The final scene stalls on arithmetic just before the hook
- **What a reader experiences:** scene 6 opens with about 18 lines of money: shirt and trouser prices, "seventy-two dollars, plus tax" (581–587), Mom counting bills (589), wage math "Eight dollars an hour, nine hours, six days. / Four hundred and thirty-two dollars a week" (607–609), the payback plan (611–613), then the bracelet (617–623). Only after all that does Chris reach the gate. The chapter's strongest pull comes after the interview (Amber in uniform, the second-needle fantasy). Here it drops into bookkeeping right when it should be climbing toward the ending.
- **Likely cause:** three money beats (the clothes debt, the wage math, the bracelet) are each set up at full length, side by side.
- **Suggested remedy:** keep the debt as one image (Mom's lips on every bill, 589) and the bracelet (623 is the real setup for your later "gift for Mom" debt). Cut or compress the wage math to one line. Optionally move the bracelet beat earlier, into the Mom scene or the walk home from the interview, so the first day enters later.
- **Confidence:** medium.

### 3. The ending announces chapter 2 three times, and once explains itself
- **What a reader experiences:** the hook comes three times: the cool kitchen "very far away" (650–658), Ms. Ravensworth's speech "Some of them find it too hard … we will find out together what else you might be good for" (674–681), and the clean nails "nothing under them at all" (695). Then line 693, "I scrubbed them last night without thinking about it, the way you do before a first day," explains the image before the reader can take it in. The critic persona penalizes that explanation. The core reader wants to know more than Chris knows, and gets that, but three signals plus an explanation turn foreshadowing into announcement.
- **Likely cause:** I was told to end on a hook toward chapter 2, so I stacked one hook for each upcoming beat.
- **Suggested remedy:** cut 693. Consider ending Ms. Ravensworth's speech at "what else you might be good for" and dropping "Some of them find it too hard" (674), which her next line already says. Keep the kitchen glimpse and the nails; together they're enough.
- **Confidence:** medium-low. Genre readers may welcome the louder signal. This is a judgment call for you.
